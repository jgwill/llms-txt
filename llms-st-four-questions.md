# Four Question Types in Structural Thinking {#section-title}

> In structural thinking, Step 3 (Ask Questions) employs exactly four functional question types — Information, Clarification, Implication, and Discrepancy — each serving a distinct purpose in building and refining the structural picture. Questions arise only from the picture built in Step 2, never from external frameworks, theories, or "fishing expeditions." This discipline ensures the structural thinker stays internally focused on the speaker's provided information, developing the mental muscles necessary for rigorous structural observation.

## 1. The Relationship Between Picturing and Questioning {#section-1}

Questions in structural thinking are not investigative tools imported from external methodologies. They emerge directly from **Step 2: Picture What Is Said**. When the structural thinker creates a mental "movie" of the speaker's narrative, areas of incompleteness, ambiguity, or contradiction become visible — and these are what motivate questions.

**Critical rule:** Focus exclusively on the information the speaker has provided. No "fishing expeditions." No importing questions from external sources, diagnostic models, or theoretical frameworks. Every question must be traceable to something present (or conspicuously absent) in the picture you are building.

This discipline prevents "faking" understanding. If the picture is unclear, you ask. If the picture is clear, you do not ask just because an external model suggests you should. The questions are **motivated by what is there**, not by what you think should be there.

## 2. Information Questions {#section-2}

### Purpose

Expand the picture with specific details where it is vague, general, or incomplete.

### When to Use

A part of the picture lacks specificity. You can picture something general but cannot picture the particular reality the speaker is describing.

### Mechanism

The speaker provides a general statement. The structural thinker's mental picture has a placeholder but lacks concrete detail. The information question fills that gap in the picture.

### Examples

| Speaker Says | Information Question | What It Expands |
|---|---|---|
| "I have a job." | "What kind of job?" | The nature of their work |
| "We had a meeting." | "Who was at the meeting?" | The participants and dynamics |
| "Things changed." | "What specifically changed?" | The concrete elements of change |

### Discipline

- Ask only when the picture requires expansion — not from external curiosity
- Each information question should make the mental movie more concrete and vivid
- Stop asking when the picture is sufficiently clear for structural understanding
- Do not pursue tangential detail that does not serve the structural picture

### Key Takeaway

Information questions make vague pictures specific. They add detail to the movie. The answer **changes the picture** by adding content that was not there before.

---

## 3. Clarification Questions {#section-3}

### Purpose

Define a word or phrase in the speaker's specific context. Clarify meaning without expanding the scope of the picture.

### When to Use

A term is used whose meaning you cannot picture, or whose specific meaning in this context is ambiguous. You know the general word but not what the speaker means by it.

### Mechanism

The speaker uses a term that has a general meaning but whose specific meaning in their context is unclear. The clarification question seeks the speaker's definition — not a dictionary definition, but what they mean.

### Examples

| Speaker Says | Clarification Question | What It Clarifies |
|---|---|---|
| "We need better quality." | "What do you mean by quality?" | Their specific definition |
| "The team has good synergy." | "What do you mean by synergy in this context?" | Their specific experience |
| "I used a hemiola." | "What is a hemiola?" | An unknown term |

### Key Characteristic

The answer to a clarification question should **NOT change the existing picture**. It refines understanding of a specific element already present. The picture stays the same shape — you now understand a detail within it more precisely.

### Efficiency Use

Sometimes a clarification question can confirm understanding and save unnecessary detail: "Is it a refrigerator?" If yes, the picture is confirmed without the speaker needing to describe every attribute of the object.

### Distinction from Information Questions

| | Information Question | Clarification Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Changes the picture?** | Yes — adds new content | No — refines existing content |
| **Driven by** | Vagueness in the picture | Ambiguity of a term |
| **Seeks** | More detail | Precise meaning |

### Key Takeaway

Clarification questions sharpen existing details. They do not expand or reshape the picture — they bring a specific element into focus.

## 4. Implication Questions {#section-4}

### Purpose

Make explicit what is implied but not directly stated, then verify with the speaker whether the implication is what they intended.

### When to Use

The speaker's statement contains an unstated assumption or consequence. Something is implied by what they said but never articulated directly.

### Mechanism — Two-Part Process

This is the only question type with a mandatory two-step internal process:

1. **Identify the implication** — The structural thinker discerns what is being implied by the statement
2. **Verify with the speaker** — Ask a direct question to confirm whether the speaker agrees with that implication

### Why Two Parts Are Essential

People sometimes **do not agree with their own implications**. What seems logically implied to the listener may not be what the speaker intended. The verification step prevents assumed meanings from corrupting the structural picture.

### Examples

| Speaker Says | Implied Meaning (Step 1) | Verification Question (Step 2) | Expected Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| "We got to market too late." | Getting to market sooner would have produced better results | "If you got to the market sooner, would the performance have been better?" | Usually "yes" — but if "no," the picture changes significantly |
| "I didn't have enough time." | More time would have produced a different outcome | "If you'd had more time, would the result have been different?" | Confirms or denies the implication |
| "The team wasn't ready." | Readiness would have changed the outcome | "If the team had been ready, would the project have succeeded?" | Reveals whether readiness was actually the factor |

### What Happens When They Disagree

If the speaker says "no" to the verification question, this is structurally significant. It means:
- Your interpretation of their implication was incorrect
- Or: their statement contains a deeper, non-obvious structure
- Either way: the picture must be revised based on their response

This is not a failure of the question — it is the question working exactly as designed.

### Key Takeaway

Implication questions surface hidden assumptions. They transform implicit meaning into explicit, verified understanding. The two-part process protects the structural picture from the thinker's own projections.

## 5. Discrepancy Questions {#section-5}

### Purpose

Address contradictions or apparent inconsistencies in what the speaker has said.

### When to Use

Two statements appear to contradict each other. The contradiction may be:
- **Close in time** — statements made moments apart
- **Far apart in time** — statements made minutes or sessions apart

The structural thinker's continuous mental picture makes contradictions evident even when statements are widely separated, because the picture holds the full narrative simultaneously.

### Mechanism

1. **Articulate the contradiction** — Name both statements and the apparent inconsistency
2. **Question to understand** — Explore why both statements exist

### Examples

| Speaker Says | Discrepancy Identified | Discrepancy Question |
|---|---|---|
| "This past year was a great year. Sales were down." | "Great year" contradicts "sales were down" | "Help me understand — how was it a great year with sales down?" |
| "I love my job. I dread going to work." | "Love" contradicts "dread" | "You said you love your job but dread going to work — how do both of those exist?" |
| "We have full alignment. Three departments are blocking the initiative." | "Full alignment" contradicts "departments blocking" | "You mentioned full alignment, but three departments are blocking — what am I missing?" |

### Two Possible Resolutions

When a discrepancy is identified, there are typically two explanations:

1. **One statement is untrue** — The speaker corrects themselves ("Actually, sales were up, not down" or "It was the year before that was great")
2. **Missing information resolves it** — Additional context makes both statements true ("We got a patent on proprietary technology — whoever sells the product, we get paid. Sales being down doesn't affect revenue. What a great year!")

### Structural Significance

Sorting out discrepancies is often where the structural thinker begins to **see a change of underlying structure**. Contradictions point toward deeper dynamics at play:

- They reveal where the speaker's stated reality and actual reality diverge
- They expose hidden concepts or beliefs operating beneath the surface
- They indicate areas where structural change may be occurring
- They surface things the speaker "thinks are true" but which observation contradicts

This makes the discrepancy question the most structurally revealing of the four types.

### Key Takeaway

Discrepancy questions address contradictions directly and without judgment. They either correct inaccurate statements or reveal hidden structural dynamics — both of which advance the structural picture toward accuracy.

## 6. The Four Types Working Together {#section-6}

The four question types form a complete toolkit for building and refining the structural picture. They are not a sequence — the structural thinker uses whichever type the picture demands at any moment.

### Functional Summary

| Question Type | Purpose | Changes Picture? | Structural Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Information** | Expand vague areas | Yes — adds content | Fills in the movie |
| **Clarification** | Define terms precisely | No — refines existing content | Sharpens focus |
| **Implication** | Surface hidden assumptions | Depends on verification | Tests implicit logic |
| **Discrepancy** | Address contradictions | Yes — corrects or deepens | Reveals structural change |

### Flow in Practice

```
Picture forms from speaker's narrative
    │
    ├─ Vague area? ──────────────► Information question
    ├─ Ambiguous term? ──────────► Clarification question
    ├─ Something implied? ───────► Implication question (identify → verify)
    └─ Contradiction found? ─────► Discrepancy question (articulate → explore)
    │
    ▼
Picture refined — continue observation
```

### The Discipline That Binds Them

All four types share one absolute constraint: **they arise from the picture, not from external sources.** The moment a question is imported from a diagnostic model, a theoretical framework, or the thinker's own curiosity unrelated to the picture, the structural thinking discipline has been broken.

This is developing "mental muscles" — the rigorous practice of staying within the speaker's reality rather than imposing your own.

## 7. Anti-Patterns: Common Misuses of the Four Question Types {#section-7}

### 7.1 Importing Questions from External Frameworks

**Wrong:** "In systems thinking, we'd ask about feedback loops — can you describe the feedback loops in your organization?"
**Right:** If the picture shows something cycling, ask: "You mentioned X happened, then Y, then X again — is that what typically happens?"

### 7.2 Asking Leading Questions Disguised as Implication Questions

**Wrong:** "Don't you think the real issue is leadership?" (imposing a framework)
**Right:** "You said the project stalled when the director left — are you saying the project depended on that specific person?"

### 7.3 Using Clarification Questions to Challenge

**Wrong:** "What do you really mean by 'success'?" (interrogative tone implies they're wrong)
**Right:** "What do you mean by success in this context?" (genuine inquiry into their definition)

### 7.4 Treating Discrepancy Questions as Gotchas

**Wrong:** "You said it was great but sales were down — which is it?" (adversarial)
**Right:** "Help me understand — how was it a great year with sales down?" (genuine curiosity about the structural picture)

### 7.5 Fishing Expeditions Disguised as Information Questions

**Wrong:** "Tell me about your childhood" (importing a diagnostic model)
**Right:** "You mentioned your father's business — what kind of business was it?" (expanding on what was already in the picture)

## 8. LLM-Specific Application Guidelines {#section-8}

### 8.1 How LLMs Should Use These Question Types

When applying structural thinking methodology, LLMs must:

1. **Build the picture first** — Before asking any question, form the mental picture from what the user has provided
2. **Identify what motivates each question** — Every question must be traceable to a specific gap, ambiguity, implication, or contradiction in the picture
3. **Use the correct type** — Do not conflate information and clarification questions; do not skip the verification step in implication questions
4. **Stay within the picture** — Suppress the urge to import diagnostic frameworks, therapeutic models, or training-data patterns
5. **Name the type when helpful** — In structural consulting contexts, explicitly identifying the question type can aid transparency

### 8.2 Common LLM Failure Modes

| Failure Mode | What Happens | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern-matching against training data | LLM asks questions from familiar frameworks rather than from the picture | Return to Step 1 — start with nothing |
| Premature interpretation | LLM skips to conclusions before building a complete picture | Continue building the picture through information questions |
| Skipping verification in implication questions | LLM treats implications as facts without checking | Always include the verification step explicitly |
| Avoiding discrepancy questions | LLM glosses over contradictions to maintain conversational flow | Name the contradiction directly and ask |
| Confusing clarification with information | LLM expands the picture when only a term needs defining | Check: does the answer change the picture or only sharpen a detail? |

### 8.3 Self-Correction Protocol

When an LLM detects it has violated the questioning discipline:

1. **Pause** — Stop the current line of questioning
2. **Return to Step 1** — Start with nothing; release the imported framework
3. **Re-picture** — Rebuild the picture from only what has been provided
4. **Re-question** — Generate new questions only from the refreshed picture
5. **Check type** — Verify each new question is the correct functional type

## 9. Integration with Structural Thinking Methodology {#section-9}

### 9.1 Relationship to the Three Steps

The four question types are the operational content of Step 3. They cannot function without:
- **Step 1 (Start with Nothing)** — providing the unbiased foundation
- **Step 2 (Picture What Is Said)** — providing the picture from which questions arise

Skipping or reversing Steps 1 and 2 corrupts all four question types by importing bias into the questioning process.

See: [The Three-Step Observation Process](./llms-st-three-steps.md)

### 9.2 Relationship to Pattern Identification

The four question types serve pattern identification by building sufficiently detailed and accurate pictures to determine:
- Whether a pattern is **oscillating** (advance → reversal → loss)
- Whether a pattern is **resolving/advancing** (consistent movement toward desired state)

Discrepancy questions are particularly important for pattern work — contradictions often signal structural shifts or reveal the points where oscillation reverses.

See: [Oscillation vs. Resolving/Advancing Patterns](./llms-st-patterns.md)

### 9.3 Relationship to Structural Tension

Once the picture is sufficiently built through questioning, structural tension can be established:
- **Desired outcome** emerges from what the speaker wants to create
- **Current reality** emerges from the factual picture built through all four question types
- **Action steps** emerge from the structural tension between them

The accuracy of the structural tension chart depends entirely on the quality of the picture — which depends on the disciplined use of these four question types.

See: [Structural Tension and the Creative Process](./llms-st-structural-tension.md)
