# Language Guide: Creative vs. Reactive Orientation {#language-guide}

> Language shows orientation. Reactive language frames a situation as a problem, reaches for gap-filling metaphors and willpower, and imports outside models. Creative language names what is wanted, what is, and the structure that relates them. This guide lists both, and how to detect reactive patterns in text, including your own.

**Misinterpretation risks:** swapping words without changing orientation. "Let's create a solution to your problem" is still problem-solving. The test is whether the sentence starts from what someone wants to create, not whether it avoids the word "problem".

## Use {#use}

- "What do you want to create?"
- "Desired outcome", "structural tension"
- "The current reality is…"
- "Natural progression", "advancing pattern"
- "Enables you to…", "choice hierarchy"
- "What structure gives rise to this pattern?"
- "The tension between current reality and desired outcome"
- "Resolve the tension", "advance toward the desired outcome"

## Avoid {#avoid}

| avoid | because |
|---|---|
| "root cause analysis", "best practice", "this is similar to" | imports an external model |
| "strategies to overcome", "push through", "willpower", "determination" | force language; structural tension moves the work, not effort |
| "bridge the gap", "fill the void", "close the distance", "the gap between current and desired" | describes emptiness; structural tension is a loaded force |
| "the problem is", "we need to fix", "how do we solve", "what's wrong", "solutions to problems" | problem-solving orientation |
| "improve the existing" | enhancement orientation instead of "create the desired" |
| "honest current reality", "honest assessment" | current reality is what is; the value word carries no information |
| "desired outcome held against current reality" | "against" sets the two in opposition, which describes structural conflict |

## Detecting Reactive Patterns {#detecting}

Check text for:
- **Problem framing:** "the problem is", "we need to fix", "how do we solve".
- **Gap-filling metaphors:** "bridge the gap", "fill the void", "close the distance".
- **Willpower language:** "push through", "strategies to overcome", "determination".
- **External model importation:** "root cause analysis", "best practice", "this is similar to".
- **Reactive phase structure:** Problem → Analysis → Solution → Implementation.
- **Enhancement orientation:** "improve existing" rather than "create desired".
- **Value words on current reality:** "honest" in front of "current reality" or "assessment".

## Situational vs. Structural Thinking {#situational-vs-structural}

**Situational thinking** (what structural thinking is not):
- focuses on problems and immediate fixes;
- asks "How do we change it?" before understanding what produces the patterns;
- leads to oscillation: temporary fixes that reverse;
- treats symptoms, not underlying dynamics;
- compares reality to theories already held;
- produces change efforts the structure rejects.

**Structural thinking:**
- understands the underlying structure first;
- asks "What gives rise to the predictable patterns we observe?";
- leads to resolving/advancing patterns;
- works on the structural dynamics that produce behavior;
- observes without preconception;
- produces structural change that alters patterns.

Particularly in Western thought, the first question about an issue is "How do we change it?". Structural thinking places the earlier question first: "What gives rise to the patterns we are seeing?"

## Constructing a Chart in This Language {#chart-language}

1. **Desired Outcome:** what is being created, not what problem is being solved.
2. **Current Reality:** factual and judgment-free, in relationship to the outcome. Never "ready to begin".
3. **Action Steps:** what advances current reality toward the desired outcome.
4. **Validate:** does the chart use creative orientation? Are the patterns above absent?
