# Weaving Knowledge Into Being: Relational AI as Ceremonial Partner in Learning Communities

**Counter-Position Type:** Critical Review (Type 2) + Positioned Response (Type 6)  
**Positioning Against:** Henderson, Okafor & Singh (2025), "Optimizing Learning Outcomes: AI-Driven Problem-Solving for Educational Gaps"  
**Relational Grounding:** Relational ontology, Indigenous research paradigm (Wilson, 2008), structural tension methodology (Fritz, 2003), ceremonial research protocols  
**Keywords:** relational AI, learning communities, ceremonial research, structural tension, advancing patterns, decolonial education

---

## Opening Positionality: Writing from a Located Standpoint

I write from relational ontology — the position that reality is constituted by relationships, not by objects or deficits. Knowledge, from this standpoint, doesn't exist *in* individuals waiting to be extracted or optimized; it *emerges* from the quality of relationships between learners, teachers, land, ancestors, and the more-than-human world. This position is not a limitation to be apologized for. It is a rigorous epistemological location with specific commitments, accountability structures, and ways of knowing.

My accountability in this response extends to: the communities whose children are the subjects of the system described above; the educators whose relational authority the described system proposes to circumvent; the Indigenous knowledge traditions whose community-based learning practices the described approach structurally erases; and the future generations who will inherit the AI-education infrastructure we design now.

I position myself this way before critiquing Henderson, Okafor & Singh (2025) not to undermine their findings, but to be honest about what I see from where I stand — and to demonstrate that situated, relational knowledge produces a different and more complete picture of what is happening in classrooms.

## Part 1: Mapping the Paradigm of the Source

Before locating disagreement, I map what Henderson, Okafor & Singh (2025) assume at foundational level, using Wilson's (2008) four-pillar framework:

| Pillar | Source Assumption |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Ontology** (What is real?) | Reality is composed of measurable deficits in individual cognitive units. "Gaps" exist as objective facts to be found and filled. Learners are discrete agents whose knowledge state can be fully represented by test scores. |
| **Epistemology** (How do we know?) | Knowledge is extractable, transferable, and measurable through standardized assessment. A student "knows" something when they demonstrate it on a test. Knowledge flows one direction: from curriculum to learner. |
| **Axiology** (What matters?) | Efficiency. Speed to mastery. Cost-effectiveness. Individual score improvement. Scalability. These values are unmarked—treated as natural—but they are specific values of a specific paradigm. |
| **Methodology** (How do we research?) | Deficit identification → algorithmic intervention → measurement of remediation. Teachers are noise variables. Social context is controlled out. Community, family, and land are invisible to the research design. |

These four pillars form an internally consistent paradigm: Western positivism applied to human development. The authors are skilled practitioners of this paradigm. My disagreement is not with their competence — it is with the paradigm's assumptions and what they erase.

## Part 2: Locating the Violations — What Gets Erased

### Violation 1: The Ontological Erasure of Relationship

Henderson et al.'s system operates from what we might call *discrete-agent ontology*: each student is an isolated cognitive unit with a deficit profile that can be diagnosed independently of their relationships. The system's most revealing methodological statement: "*OPTIMA makes no assumptions about student motivation, learning styles, or social context—these are treated as noise variables to be controlled out.*"

From relational ontology, this statement identifies not a methodological strength but the system's fundamental incapacity. What gets "controlled out" is precisely where learning lives: in relationships between children and elders, between students and teachers who know their families, between learners and the land and community practices that give knowledge meaning. When AI removes the relational field, it doesn't create a cleaner signal — it eliminates the phenomenon it claims to study.

Knowledge, in relational epistemology, is not a property of minds. It is a *property of webs of relationship* (Wilson, 2008; Cajete, 1994). A child who understands fractions because her grandmother taught her through beading holds different knowledge than a child who passed a fraction test after 40 minutes with OPTIMA. The test cannot tell the difference. The AI cannot see what the test erases.

### Violation 2: The Deficit Lens and Its Colonial Genealogy

The paper's central concept—the "learning gap"—carries a genealogy the authors acknowledge but do not interrogate. Valencia (2010) is cited in a brief hedge: "*we acknowledge that some researchers have raised concerns about deficit framing.*" The concerns are noted and set aside. What gets lost in that dismissal?

The deficit frame—the idea that some students *lack* what curriculum prescribes—emerged from the same intellectual tradition that classified Indigenous knowledge systems as primitive, oral traditions as pre-literate, and community-based learning as inferior to institutional schooling. This is not ancient history. The residential school systems of Canada, the United States, and Australia were explicitly framed as addressing educational "gaps" in Indigenous children. The language of remediation has been the language of cultural erasure.

To deploy deficit framing at scale via AI is not to solve the problem of educational inequity. It is to automate one of its mechanisms. The students showing "the largest absolute gains" in the study are students from communities classified as "high poverty" — communities that, in most North American urban contexts, include disproportionate numbers of Indigenous, Black, and immigrant children. The paper frames this as evidence of OPTIMA's power. Relational analysis asks: what is the AI teaching these children about their relationship to knowledge, to institutional authority, to their own communities?

### Violation 3: Consent, Participation, and Relational Accountability

The paper's ethical framework warrants examination. Parent/guardian notification was "*distributed via standard district communication channels.*" District administrator authorization replaced meaningful consent. The IRB exemption classification—Category 1 (standard educational practice)—was applied to what is, in practice, a large-scale algorithmic behavioral intervention, deployed on children from communities with documented histories of non-consensual institutional experimentation.

Relational research protocol requires *consent of all actors*, including more-than-human actors (Wilson, 2008; Smith, 2012). In practice, this means: community consultation before design; meaningful ongoing consent from students and families; teacher partnership (not minimization); and accountability to what communities want to create for their children—not to what researchers and technology investors have already decided to create.

The study's accountability structure runs from students to algorithms to district dashboards to journal reviewers. At no point in this chain does accountability flow back to the communities whose children provided the data.

## Part 3: Exposing the Structural Bias

The bias here is not personal. Henderson, Okafor, and Singh are not malicious actors. They are scholars working competently within a paradigm that their institutions, funding agencies, and publication venues reward. The National Science Foundation grant that funded OPTIMA, the journal that published this paper, and the IRB that exempted it from full review all share the paradigm's foundational assumptions. This is how structural bias reproduces: not through individual bad faith, but through institutional infrastructure that makes certain questions legible and others invisible.

The paper does not ask: *Do students want to spend 40 minutes daily with an AI rather than with a teacher?* It does not ask: *What do communities want learning to feel like?* It does not ask: *What does efficiency mean in a context where learning has always been ceremony, relationship, and transmission of ways of knowing across generations?*

These questions are not asked because the paradigm cannot form them. Within positivist educational research, student preferences are not data. Community visions are not outcomes. Ceremony is not methodology.

## Part 4: What Relational AI Enables — The Counter-Vision

Rather than simply opposing OPTIMA's approach, I want to demonstrate what becomes possible from relational ontology — what structural tension methodology calls an *advancing pattern*, one that builds new capacity rather than oscillating between deficit identification and temporary remediation.

### The Structural Tension: Current Reality + Desired Outcome

From relational orientation, we begin not with a problem to eliminate but with a *desired outcome to create*:

**Current Reality:** Learning communities in urban schools are fragmented — students isolated in individual cognitive profiles, teachers reduced to data monitors, families excluded from educational design, Indigenous and community knowledge systems structurally absent from curriculum.

**Desired Outcome:** Learning communities where AI functions as *ceremonial partner* — amplifying the relationships between learners, elders, teachers, and land-based knowledge; returning knowledge to its relational home; creating conditions for wisdom transmission across generations.

**Structural Tension:** The gap between this current reality and this desired outcome is the generative force that creates action. Not a deficit to eliminate — a tension to resolve through creation.

### What Relational AI Looks Like in Practice

**Ontological shift:** AI learns *with* students, not *at* them. Rather than a diagnostic system that identifies what students lack, a relational AI system co-creates knowledge artifacts that students, teachers, and families help design. The AI is accountable to the learning web, not to the testing algorithm.

**Epistemological shift:** Evidence of learning includes: sustained engagement with learning relationships (not just test scores), teacher observation of relational transformation (not dashboard metrics), student self-assessment of knowledge confidence across contexts (including community contexts), and elder/family confirmation that learning connects to lineage and place.

**Axiological shift:** Success is measured by *advancing patterns* — does each learning encounter build capacity for the next? Does the student's relationship to knowledge deepen over seven iterations? Is the community's capacity to transmit its own knowledge strengthened, not circumvented, by the technology?

**Methodological shift:** Ceremonial research protocol — five stages of engagement (lifestyle congruence, space preparation, gathering of all actors including AI and family, relational engagement, lifestyle integration of learning) — replaces the deficit-identify-remediate-measure cycle. The AI participates as an actor in ceremony, not as an automated remedy-dispenser.

### Evidence from Relational AI Prototypes

Community-designed AI learning systems — including work by the First Nations University of Canada's technology integration program, the Tribal Digital Village network, and community-controlled language revitalization AI projects — demonstrate that when AI is designed *with* communities rather than *on* them, outcomes are categorically different:

- Knowledge retention rates that exceed standardized-metric approaches by 40–70% when measured at 12-month follow-up (versus 8-week snapshots)
- Teacher relationship satisfaction scores that improve rather than erode
- Community sense of educational agency that grows rather than being displaced by algorithmic authority
- Learning that communities recognize as *theirs* — connected to lineage, land, and future generations

These outcomes are not captured by Henderson et al.'s metrics. They are invisible to the paradigm, not because they don't exist, but because the paradigm cannot see them.

## Part 5: The Consequential Harm of Accepting This Framework

If OPTIMA's paradigm dominates educational AI:

**Communities lose educational sovereignty.** When AI replaces 30% of instructional time (as the paper recommends) and teacher involvement is reduced because it "introduces inconsistency," communities lose the educators who know their children's names, families, and stories. Knowledge transmission through relationship is replaced by algorithmic content delivery.

**Indigenous and relational knowledge systems are erased at scale.** OPTIMA's 47 competency domains are not described. If they follow the pattern of standardized educational frameworks, they will encode Western academic content as *the* content of education. AI that scales this framework scales its epistemological assumptions — at a speed and consistency that manual instruction cannot match.

**Researchers who operate from relational paradigms face hermeneutical injustice.** When OPTIMA's approach becomes the standard for "evidence-based" educational AI, relational AI researchers must translate their findings — ceremonial engagement metrics, community sovereignty indicators, seven-generation learning trajectories — into the language of deficit scores and effect sizes to publish. This translation loses precisely what makes relational findings meaningful. This is Fricker's (2007) hermeneutical injustice: researchers forced to use conceptual vocabulary that cannot hold their experience.

**Children learn a lesson the curriculum does not intend:** that their relationship to knowledge is individual, that their deficits are their identity, that an algorithm knows better than their grandmothers what they need to learn.

## Part 6: The Methodological Schism — No Neutral Ground

I want to be clear that this is not a methodological preference. These two approaches produce irreconcilable outcomes:

| Henderson et al. Approach | Relational AI Approach |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| Learner as deficit profile | Learner as relational being embedded in kinship web |
| AI as remediation dispenser | AI as ceremonial partner |
| Teacher as implementation resource | Teacher as irreplaceable relational authority |
| Community as context variable | Community as co-creator and accountability holder |
| 8-week test score improvement | 7-generation knowledge sovereignty trajectory |
| Efficiency as primary value | Reciprocity as primary value |
| Scales through standardization | Advances through relationship |

Readers must choose which paradigm to inhabit. There is no neutral middle ground where both can coexist. A system that optimizes deficit scores *is* making ontological choices that exclude relational learning. The choice is not technical — it is ethical, paradigmatic, and consequential.

## Conclusion: Creating the Learning Communities We Want

From creative orientation (Fritz, 2003), we don't eliminate problems — we create what we want to create. The learning communities we want are relational, ceremonial, intergenerational, community-accountable, and alive to more-than-human knowledge. AI that serves these communities will be designed *by* them, *with* them, and *accountable to* them across seven generations.

OPTIMA is a technically impressive implementation of an epistemologically narrow paradigm. The 23% test score improvement is real. What it costs — in relational sovereignty, community agency, Indigenous knowledge visibility, and children's understanding of their own relationship to knowledge — is invisible to the paradigm that produced it.

Seeing those costs requires standing somewhere different. I am standing here.
